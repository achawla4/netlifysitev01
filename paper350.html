The Born Perceptron: Mimicking Quantum Mechanics and Distributed Measurements in Time

Chawla, Aman

Abstract

—This paper develops a neural architecture capable of reproducing several structural aspects of quantum mechanics. The proposed model, termed the Born Perceptron, extends the classical perceptron by structuring outputs as value–index pairs and interpreting normalized activations as probability distributions over indexed outcomes. By allowing indices to inhabit geometric manifolds rather than discrete labels, the perceptron naturally represents probabilistic sampling over state spaces. When stacked sequentially, such perceptrons generate stochastic trajectories reminiscent of quantum state evolution under repeated measurement. Introducing complex-valued ac- tivations yields probabilities determined by squared magnitudes, reproducing the Born rule. The framework is further extended to treat measurements distributed in time, particularly position samplings. In this regime, the neural system produces sequences of sampled positions whose joint probabilities resemble those derived in path-integral formulations of quantum mechanics. The resulting architecture offers a classical computational model that captures several conceptual features of quantum theory including measurement, collapse, trajectory evolution, and amplitude-based probability rules. I. INTRODUCTION Quantum mechanics is fundamentally probabilistic. The outcome of a measurement cannot in general be predicted deterministically from the quantum state. Instead, measure- ment results appear as random samples drawn from probability distributions determined by the Born rule. For a quantum state represented by a density matrix ρ and a measurement described by a positive operator-valued measure (POVM) Ek, the probability of observing outcome k is given by pk = Tr(Ekρ). (1) This probabilistic structure bears a striking resemblance to the behavior of classical machine learning models that produce normalized probability distributions. Neural networks routinely map input vectors to collections of scores that are normalized into probabilities using functions such as softmax. While such models were not originally designed to mimic quantum measurement, their mathematical structure provides an intriguing analogy. The present work explores whether a simple neural prim- itive, the perceptron, can be structured to emulate several aspects of quantum measurement and evolution. We introduce the concept of the Born Perceptron, a perceptron whose outputs are interpreted as probability distributions over indexed outcomes. By pairing outputs into value–index structures and allowing the indices to reside in geometric manifolds, the model can represent distributions over state spaces. Sampling from these distributions corresponds to a form of collapse analogous to quantum measurement. When such perceptron layers are stacked sequentially, the sampled outputs propagate through the network and generate stochastic trajectories across the manifold of states. This behavior parallels descriptions of quantum trajectories in open quantum systems. By further extending the perceptron to support complex-valued activations, the squared magnitude of the resulting amplitudes determines probabilities, reproducing the mathematical structure of the Born rule. The framework is extended to address measurements dis- tributed in time. In particular, we consider the problem of sequential position sampling of a quantum particle. Path- integral formulations of quantum mechanics demonstrate that joint probabilities for sequences of measurement outcomes can be expressed in terms of integrals over all possible system trajectories. We show that a neural architecture built from stacked Born perceptrons can generate analogous joint distributions through sequential sampling and propagation. II. STRUCTURED OUTPUTS AND THE PAIRING PRINCIPLE Traditional perceptrons compute a vector of activations vk = wk · x + bk (2) for an input vector x. In standard classification tasks the pre- dicted class corresponds to the index of the largest activation. In the Born perceptron this interpretation is modified. Instead of treating outputs as mere scores associated with categorical labels, each activation is paired with an explicit index. The output of the perceptron therefore takes the form (v1, i1), (v2, i2), . . . , (vn, in). (3) The values vk are normalized into probabilities pk = evk P j evj . (4) The perceptron thus defines a conditional probability distri- bution over indices x →p(ik|x). (5) Sampling from this distribution produces a single index ik. This process resembles measurement collapse in the sense that a probabilistic distribution is reduced to a single realized outcome.

The pairing principle emphasizes that the perceptron output contains two distinct components. The value determines the probability weight associated with an outcome, while the index specifies the identity of that outcome. By explicitly repre- senting both components, the perceptron can be interpreted as a probabilistic generator of outcomes rather than merely a classifier. III. INDICES AS ELEMENTS OF GEOMETRIC MANIFOLDS In conventional neural classifiers indices correspond to discrete labels. The Born perceptron generalizes this notion by allowing indices to occupy positions in a geometric space. Instead of treating ik as an abstract label, we interpret it as a vector ik ∈Rd. (6) The output structure therefore becomes (vk, ik) (7) with probabilities p(ik|x). (8) Sampling selects a point from a discrete set embedded within a continuous manifold. The perceptron therefore de- fines a probability distribution over candidate points in that manifold. A useful example arises in the representation of qubit states. A qubit can be described by a Bloch vector r = (rx, ry, rz) (9) lying within the Bloch sphere. If candidate indices correspond to points inside this sphere, the perceptron can generate dis- tributions over possible quantum states. Sampling then selects a particular state vector. In this interpretation the perceptron maps an input vector into a distribution over geometric states. The sampling process transforms the input into another point in the manifold, which can subsequently be propagated through further neural layers. IV. RELATIONSHIP WITH POVM MEASUREMENTS Quantum measurement probabilities exhibit a linear depen- dence on the quantum state. If the density matrix is vectorized into a real parameter vector x, the probability rule can be written as pk = wk · x. (10) This expression has precisely the same structure as a percep- tron activation. Consequently, a perceptron can approximate the mapping from quantum states to measurement probabilities by learning appropriate weights. Consider a qubit measured along the z axis. The probabil- ities of obtaining spin up or spin down are p↑= 1 + rz , p↓= 1 −rz . (11) A perceptron with weights aligned along the z direction reproduces this mapping. The correspondence highlights how the perceptron naturally implements linear probability rules similar to those governing POVM measurements. V. STACKING LAYERS AND STOCHASTIC TRAJECTORIES A single Born perceptron layer generates a probability distribution over indices and produces a sampled outcome. When such layers are arranged sequentially, the sampled outcome from one layer becomes the input to the next. If xt denotes the state produced by layer t, the next layer samples a new index according to xt+1 ∼p(i|xt). (12) The resulting evolution x0 →x1 →x2 →. . . (13) defines a stochastic trajectory across the manifold of indices. The probability of observing a particular trajectory is given by P(path) = Y t P(xt+1|xt). (14) This structure is mathematically equivalent to a Markov chain. In the context of quantum measurement it resembles quantum trajectory descriptions in which a quantum system evolves stochastically under repeated observation. VI. COMPLEX ACTIVATIONS AND THE BORN RULE A closer analogy with quantum mechanics emerges when perceptron activations are allowed to be complex numbers. Let zk = wk · x (15) with zk ∈C. Probabilities are then obtained through squared magnitudes pk = |zk|2 P j |zj|2 . (16) This expression is identical in form to the Born rule used in quantum mechanics. The activations represent probability amplitudes whose squared magnitudes determine outcome probabilities. As a simple illustration, consider complex activations z1 = 1 + i, z2 = 2 −i. (17) Their squared magnitudes are |z1|2 = 2, |z2|2 = 5. (18) The resulting probabilities are p1 = 2 7, p2 = 5 7. (19) Complex activations therefore allow perceptrons to repre- sent amplitude-based probability structures.

VII. MEASUREMENTS DISTRIBUTED IN TIME Quantum measurements frequently occur not as isolated events but as sequences distributed in time. In path-integral formulations the joint probability of a sequence of measure- ment outcomes can be expressed in terms of integrals over all possible trajectories of the system. Consider a particle whose position is sampled at times t1 < t2 < · · · < tQ. (20) The measurement results are positions x1, x2, . . . , xQ. (21) In path-integral formulations the joint amplitude associated with this sequence involves contributions from all possible paths consistent with the sampled positions. A neural architecture built from Born perceptrons can emu- late this situation by performing sequential sampling. At each time step the network generates a distribution over possible positions and samples one value. The sampled position then influences the next step of the evolution. VIII. NEURAL POSITION SAMPLING Let the state of the system at time t be represented by a neural vector st. A measurement layer computes activations corresponding to candidate positions vk = wk · st. (22) After normalization, these activations define a probability distribution over position indices xk. Sampling yields a mea- sured position xq. Following the measurement, the neural state is updated using a transformation that incorporates the measurement outcome. This transformation may be interpreted as the neural analogue of a resolution function that modifies the wavefunc- tion following a measurement. The updated state then propagates forward to the next time step, where the process repeats. In this way the network generates a sequence of sampled positions. IX. NEURAL REPRESENTATION OF PATH INTEGRALS The sequential sampling procedure described above pro- duces trajectories x1, x2, . . . , xQ (23) with probabilities determined by the product of conditional distributions generated by the perceptron layers. When activations are complex-valued, each transition car- ries a complex amplitude. The amplitude associated with an entire trajectory is obtained by multiplying the amplitudes of individual steps. Summing amplitudes across trajectories yields interference effects analogous to those found in quantum mechanics. In the limit of finely spaced time steps, the neural ar- chitecture approximates a path-integral formulation in which trajectories contribute amplitudes determined by accumulated phase factors. X. DISCUSSION The Born perceptron provides a classical computational architecture that captures several conceptual elements of quan- tum mechanics. The pairing of values and indices transforms perceptron outputs into probabilistic generators of outcomes. Allowing indices to inhabit geometric manifolds enables the model to represent distributions over state spaces rather than discrete labels. Sequential stacking of perceptron layers gener- ates stochastic trajectories across these manifolds, mimicking the evolution of quantum systems under repeated measure- ment. Complex activations introduce amplitude-based probability rules identical in form to the Born rule. When measurements are distributed in time, the resulting neural architecture pro- duces sequences of sampled positions whose joint probabilities resemble those derived from path-integral formulations. While the framework does not claim to reproduce the full physical structure of quantum theory, it offers a compelling conceptual bridge between neural computation and quantum probability. The approach suggests that several mathematical features of quantum mechanics may be interpreted as emergent properties of probabilistic sampling processes implemented by layered computational systems. XI. CONCLUSION This paper introduced the Born perceptron as a neural architecture capable of mimicking several structural features of quantum mechanics. By structuring perceptron outputs as value–index pairs and interpreting normalized activations as probability distributions, the model generates stochastic measurement outcomes analogous to those described by the Born rule. Allowing indices to occupy geometric manifolds enables the network to represent distributions over state spaces. Stacking layers of Born perceptrons produces stochastic trajectories reminiscent of quantum state evolution under re- peated observation. The introduction of complex-valued acti- vations yields probability amplitudes whose squared magni- tudes determine outcome probabilities. Extending the frame- work to measurements distributed in time enables the neural architecture to produce sequences of position samplings whose joint distributions resemble those obtained in path-integral formulations. These results suggest that neural architectures based on probabilistic sampling may provide useful conceptual tools for understanding quantum measurement and dynamics. Future work may explore more detailed connections between neural trajectory models and the mathematical structures underlying quantum theory. XII. NEURAL MEASUREMENT ARCHITECTURES AND THE BORN RULE A. Quantum Measurement as Probabilistic Sampling Quantum mechanics differs fundamentally from classical mechanics in that it does not predict deterministic outcomes for individual measurements. Instead, the theory provides probability distributions over possible outcomes. For a system

described by a density matrix ρ and a measurement repre- sented by a set of positive operators {Ek} forming a positive operator-valued measure (POVM), the probability of observing outcome k is pk = Tr(Ekρ). (24) The result of a measurement is therefore a random variable drawn from this distribution. Once an outcome is observed, the system state is updated according to the measurement postulate, a process often described as wavefunction collapse. A sequence of measurements performed at successive times produces a stochastic sequence of outcomes. These outcomes can be interpreted as forming a trajectory through the space of possible measurement results. In modern quantum theory such trajectories are used to describe continuously monitored systems and form the basis of quantum trajectory formulations of measurement dynamics. This probabilistic character suggests an intriguing con- nection with computational systems that naturally produce probability distributions. In particular, many neural network architectures generate normalized probability outputs that are subsequently sampled to produce discrete outcomes. The structural similarity between probabilistic neural inference and quantum measurement motivates the exploration of neural architectures capable of reproducing elements of quantum measurement theory. B. Probabilistic Neural Generators Neural networks routinely produce probability distributions as outputs. A simple example is the perceptron layer, which computes a set of activations vk = wk · x + bk (25) for an input vector x. These activations are typically con- verted into probabilities using a normalization such as the softmax function pk = evk P j evj . (26) Sampling from this distribution yields a discrete outcome k. In conventional machine learning tasks, the index k cor- responds to a class label. However, the perceptron can also be interpreted more generally as a probabilistic generator of outcomes. Each output unit produces a pair consisting of an activation value and an associated index, (vk, ik). (27) The value determines the probability weight, while the index identifies the outcome. Sampling selects one of the indices according to the computed probabilities. In this interpretation the perceptron acts not merely as a classifier but as a stochastic generator of discrete events. This perspective is particularly useful when modeling pro- cesses in which outcomes correspond to physical states rather than abstract labels. C. Quantum States and Measurement Operators The probabilistic nature of quantum measurement arises from the structure of quantum states and measurement opera- tors. A quantum state may be represented by a vector in Hilbert space or, more generally, by a density matrix ρ. Measurement operators act on this state and determine the probabilities of different outcomes. The mapping ρ →pk (28) transforms the quantum state into a probability distribution over measurement outcomes. This transformation is linear in the state variables when expressed in terms of expectation values. Consequently, quantum measurement can be viewed as a probabilistic map from a state space to an outcome space. This viewpoint emphasizes that measurement devices func- tion as probabilistic generators whose output distributions depend on the system state. Such generators can be represented computationally by structures that map state representations to probability distributions. Neural networks provide one possible computational real- ization of such mappings. D. The Born Perceptron Architecture The Born perceptron is a neural architecture designed to mimic structural aspects of quantum measurement. The out- puts of the perceptron are interpreted as value–index pairs (v1, i1), (v2, i2), . . . , (vn, in). (29) The activations vk are converted into probabilities p(ik|x) = evk P j evj . (30) Sampling from this distribution produces an index ik that represents the outcome of a measurement-like process. In contrast to conventional classification tasks, the indices are not restricted to discrete labels. Instead they may represent coordinates within a geometric manifold ik ∈Rd. (31) Each index therefore corresponds to a candidate point in a continuous state space. Sampling from the perceptron output selects one of these points. For example, if the indices correspond to points on the Bloch sphere, the perceptron generates a probability distribu- tion over possible qubit states. Sampling from the distribution produces a specific state vector that may be propagated to subsequent layers. This interpretation transforms the perceptron into a mecha- nism that maps an input state representation to a distribution over candidate physical states.

E. Probability Amplitudes and the Born Rule Quantum measurement probabilities are determined by probability amplitudes. If a quantum state is expanded in a basis with amplitudes ψk, the probability of observing outcome k is given by the Born rule pk = |ψk|2. (32) The use of complex amplitudes allows interference between different contributions to the state. This interference is re- sponsible for many of the distinctive features of quantum phenomena. A computational architecture that aims to reproduce the structure of quantum probabilities must therefore accommo- date complex-valued quantities whose squared magnitudes determine probabilities. F. (37) Such trajectories resemble the sequences of measurement outcomes produced when a quantum system is repeatedly observed. G. MEASUREMENT DISTURBANCE AND QUANTUM NONDEMOLITION OBSERVABLES A. Measurement Disturbance in Sequential Neural Sampling In the previous section we introduced the Born perceptron as a neural architecture capable of generating stochastic trajecto- ries through sequential sampling of probability distributions. Each layer produces an outcome that becomes the input to the next stage of the computation. This sequential sampling process naturally raises the question of how the act of sampling influences subsequent evolution. Consider a neural state vector xt representing the state of the system at step t. A measurement-like layer produces a probability distribution over possible outcomes p(i|xt) (38) and sampling from this distribution yields an index it ∼p(i|xt). (39) The sampled outcome modifies the input to the next stage, generating a new state xt+1. The evolution of the system therefore depends explicitly on the measurement result. In its most general form the update rule can be written as xt+1 = F(xt, it), (40)

where F represents the transformation implemented by the network. This dependence of future states on measurement outcomes implies that the act of sampling introduces a disturbance into the system dynamics. The disturbance arises because the sampling process selects a specific outcome from a distribution that encodes multiple possibilities. Once a particular outcome has been chosen, the subsequent trajectory is constrained by that selection. From this perspective the Born perceptron does not merely read out a state representation; it modifies the state through the act of measurement. This behavior mirrors a central feature of quantum mechanics, where measurements influence the system being observed. B. Latent State Dynamics and Disturbance Channels To analyze the structure of this disturbance it is useful to introduce an explicit representation of the latent state of the system. Let the neural state be represented by a vector st = (xt, pt), (41) where xt and pt may be interpreted as conjugate variables analogous to position and momentum in classical mechanics. Although these variables arise within a neural representation, they provide a convenient way to analyze the effects of measurement disturbance. Suppose that a measurement layer samples a position-like variable from a distribution determined by the state st. The sampling process introduces fluctuations into the conjugate variable pt. The evolution of the state can then be written as st+1 = H(st) + ηt, (42) where H represents the deterministic dynamics and ηt is a disturbance term associated with measurement. The disturbance term may be interpreted as a stochastic kick applied to the latent variables. In particular, sampling of the position coordinate introduces uncertainty into the momentum coordinate. The magnitude of the disturbance depends on the precision with which the measurement is performed. Highly precise measurements correspond to narrow probability distri- butions and therefore produce larger fluctuations in conjugate variables. This trade-off between measurement precision and distur- bance is a central feature of quantum measurement theory. C. Connection to Quantum Measurement Back-Action The disturbance mechanism described above closely resem- bles the phenomenon of measurement back-action in quantum mechanics. According to the Heisenberg uncertainty principle, position and momentum operators satisfy the commutation relation [x, p] = iℏ. (43) This relation implies that the uncertainties in position and momentum obey ∆x ∆p ≥ℏ 2. (44) As a result, any attempt to measure the position of a particle with high precision inevitably disturbs its momentum. The disturbance subsequently influences the particle’s future motion. The concept of measurement back-action plays a crucial role in precision measurement experiments. In particular, the detec- tion of extremely small displacements, such as those produced by gravitational waves, requires repeated measurements of mechanical oscillators. In such experiments the measurement process itself can introduce noise that limits the achievable sensitivity. This limitation is known as the standard quantum limit. The neural disturbance mechanism introduced above repro- duces the qualitative structure of measurement back-action. Sampling of a position-like variable generates fluctuations in the conjugate variable, which then propagate through subse- quent evolution. The resulting trajectories exhibit a stochastic character similar to those observed in quantum measurement processes. D. Emergence of Quantum Nondemolition Observables Although many observables are affected by measurement back-action, certain special observables can be monitored repeatedly without being disturbed. Such quantities are known as quantum nondemolition (QND) observables. The theory of QND measurements was developed extensively by Braginsky and collaborators in the context of gravitational-wave detec- tion. An observable A is said to be a QND observable if measurements performed at different times commute, [A(ti), A(tj)] = 0. (45) When this condition is satisfied, a measurement of A does not disturb future measurements of the same observable. The observable can therefore be monitored continuously without being destroyed by the measurement process. A well-known example occurs for a harmonic oscillator. If x(t) and p(t) denote the position and momentum of the oscillator, one may define the quadrature variables X1 = x cos ωt − p mω sin ωt (46) and X2 = x sin ωt + p mω cos ωt. (47) These variables correspond to the slowly varying amplitudes of the oscillator motion. Measurements of a single quadrature can be arranged so that the disturbance is transferred entirely to the conjugate quadrature. As a result, repeated measurements

of the chosen quadrature do not degrade its future predictabil- ity. This principle underlies a variety of back-action–evading measurement schemes. E. Neural Invariants and Back-Action Evading Variables The concept of QND observables has a natural interpretation within the neural framework. Consider a function of the latent state At = g(st). (48) If the disturbance term ηt affects only components orthog- onal to the gradient of g, then the value of At remains unchanged by the measurement process. In this case the variable At behaves as an invariant of the neural dynamics. Mathematically, the condition for invariance may be written as At+1 = At. (49) Such invariant features correspond to directions in state space that are unaffected by measurement disturbance. Mea- surements may strongly perturb other components of the state vector while leaving the invariant variable unchanged. This structure closely mirrors the behavior of QND observ- ables in quantum mechanics. The neural invariant plays the role of a measurable quantity that can be monitored repeatedly without accumulating disturbance. F. Stroboscopic Measurement Interpretation In many physical systems it is not possible to construct a continuously measurable QND observable. However, it is sometimes possible to perform measurements at specific times such that the disturbance introduced by earlier measurements does not influence subsequent observations. This technique is known as stroboscopic measurement. For a harmonic oscillator, measurements performed at in- tervals equal to half the oscillation period correspond to times when the commutator of the position operator with itself vanishes, [x(t), x(t + τ)] = 0 (50) for τ = π/ω. Under these conditions a sequence of measurements can track the oscillator position with minimal disturbance. The neural sampling framework provides a natural inter- pretation of this phenomenon. If measurement layers are activated only at specific steps in the trajectory, the disturbance introduced at one step may evolve into a direction that does not affect the measured variable at the next step. By synchronizing the measurement intervals with the dynamics of the system, it becomes possible to observe certain features repeatedly without degrading their predictability. This interpretation illustrates how the Born perceptron ar- chitecture can reproduce several qualitative aspects of quan- tum measurement theory, including measurement disturbance, back-action, and the emergence of QND observables. In the following section we extend this framework to the continuous-time limit and show how sequential neural measurements lead naturally to stochastic evolution equations closely related to the Belavkin theory of quantum filtering. XIV. CONTINUOUS MEASUREMENT AND NEURAL STOCHASTIC DYNAMICS A. Continuous Quantum Measurement Many physical measurement processes occur not as isolated discrete events but as continuous interactions between a system and a measuring device. In such situations information about the system is extracted gradually, producing a measurement record that evolves in time. Examples include optical homo- dyne detection, superconducting qubit monitoring, and the observation of mechanical oscillators in gravitational-wave detectors. The theoretical description of continuously monitored quan- tum systems differs from the instantaneous projection picture of traditional measurement theory. Instead of a single collapse event, the system state evolves stochastically in response to the continuous stream of measurement information. The state conditioned on the measurement record obeys a stochastic differential equation. Let ρt denote the density matrix of a system conditioned on the measurement outcomes up to time t. In the theory developed by Belavkin, the evolution of ρt can be written in the form dρt = −i[H, ρt]dt + D[L]ρt dt + H[L]ρt dWt, (51) where H is the Hamiltonian of the system, L is the mea- surement operator, and dWt is a Wiener noise increment. The superoperators D and H describe decoherence and stochastic state updates induced by the measurement process. The measurement record itself is described by dYt = Tr[(L + L†)ρt]dt + dWt. (52) The stochastic noise term reflects the unavoidable random- ness associated with quantum measurement. B. Discrete Neural Measurement Updates The Born perceptron architecture introduced earlier gener- ates measurement outcomes through sequential probabilistic sampling. At step t the system state is represented by a neural vector xt. A measurement layer computes probabilities p(i|xt) (53) and produces a sampled outcome it ∼p(i|xt). (54)

The sampled index is then used to update the system representation, producing the next state xt+1 = F(xt, it). (55) This update rule resembles the filtering step used in se- quential inference algorithms. The neural system maintains an internal state that is modified whenever a new observation becomes available. In this sense the perceptron layer acts as a measurement device whose outcomes influence the subsequent trajectory of the latent state. The stochastic nature of the update rule implies that the evolution of xt forms a random process. The probability of observing a particular trajectory is determined by the product of conditional probabilities generated at each step. C. Continuous-Time Limit To establish a connection with continuous measurement theory we consider the limit in which the time interval between successive measurement steps becomes very small. Let the interval between updates be denoted by ∆t. For sufficiently small ∆t, the measurement outcome can be decomposed into a deterministic component and a noise component, mt = µ(xt)∆t + √ ∆t ηt, (56) where ηt is a zero-mean random variable with unit variance. In the limit ∆t →0, the cumulative effect of the noise terms converges to a Wiener process. The sequence of measurement outcomes therefore approaches a continuous measurement record dYt = µ(xt)dt + dWt. (57) This expression has the same structure as the measurement signal appearing in the theory of continuous quantum mea- surement. The state update rule can also be expanded in powers of ∆t. Writing xt+∆t = xt + f(xt)∆t + g(xt)ηt √ ∆t, (58) and taking the limit ∆t →0 yields the stochastic differential equation dxt = f(xt)dt + g(xt)dWt. (59) The neural state therefore evolves according to a diffusion process driven by measurement noise. D. Neural Stochastic Dynamics The stochastic differential equation obtained above de- scribes the evolution of the neural state under repeated mea- surement updates. The deterministic term f(xt) represents the intrinsic dynamics of the system, while the stochastic term g(xt)dWt captures the fluctuations introduced by measure- ment. Such equations are commonly encountered in models of physical systems subject to noise. In the present context the noise arises from the sampling process used to generate measurement outcomes. If the neural state is interpreted as a representation of a physical system, the stochastic term can be viewed as the neural analogue of measurement back-action. Each measure- ment introduces a random perturbation that modifies the state trajectory. The resulting dynamics produce a family of possible tra- jectories, each corresponding to a different realization of the measurement noise. The ensemble of trajectories represents the range of possible evolutions compatible with the measurement record. E. Belavkin Filtering Equation The stochastic evolution derived above closely resembles the structure of the Belavkin filtering equation used in quantum measurement theory. In that theory the conditional density matrix evolves according to dρt = −i[H, ρt]dt + LρtL†dt −1 2{L†L, ρt}dt + H[L]ρtdWt. (60) The deterministic terms describe the Hamiltonian evolution and measurement-induced decoherence, while the stochastic term represents the random update produced by the measure- ment record. The measurement signal itself is dYt = Tr[(L + L†)ρt]dt + dWt. (61) These equations form the basis of quantum filtering theory, which describes how an observer updates their knowledge of a system based on a continuous stream of measurement outcomes. F. Measurement Records and Quantum Trajectories Continuous quantum measurement generates a time- dependent record of outcomes that reflect the interaction between a physical system and its measurement apparatus. In quantum trajectory theory the system state evolves con- ditionally on this measurement record. Each realization of the measurement signal corresponds to a stochastic trajectory of the system state. Let Y (t) denote the measurement record produced by mon- itoring a quantum observable. In the framework of continuous quantum measurement the signal may be written as dYt = Tr[(L + L†)ρt]dt + dWt, (62) where ρt is the conditional density matrix of the system and dWt is a Wiener noise increment. The first term represents the expected signal determined by the current system state, while the stochastic term represents unavoidable measurement noise. The conditional state ρt evolves according to a stochastic master equation, often referred to as the Belavkin filtering equation. The measurement record therefore provides a contin- uous stream of information that allows an observer to update their estimate of the system state. Each realization of the measurement noise produces a different trajectory of the conditional state. The ensemble of trajectories describes the possible evolutions of the system under continuous observation. B. Neural Representation of Measurement Records Within the Born perceptron framework the measurement record appears naturally as the sequence of outcomes gen- erated by the sampling process. At each time step the neural architecture produces an outcome it ∼p(i|xt) (63) which becomes part of a trajectory i1, i2, i3, . . . . (64) This sequence is directly analogous to the measurement record produced in a physical experiment. The internal neural state xt evolves in response to these observations according to the update rule xt+1 = F(xt, it). (65) From a statistical perspective the neural system maintains a latent representation of the system state that is refined whenever new measurement outcomes become available. The perceptron layers therefore implement a filtering process in which the internal state acts as an evolving estimate of the underlying physical system. The stochastic trajectories generated by the neural archi- tecture correspond to realizations of a measurement process in which both the state estimate and the measurement record evolve simultaneously. C. Belavkin Filtering as Bayesian Inference The theory of quantum filtering developed by Belavkin pro- vides a mathematical framework for describing the conditional evolution of quantum systems under continuous observation. In this theory the density matrix ρt represents the observer’s best estimate of the system state given the measurement record up to time t. The update of ρt can be interpreted as a quantum analogue of Bayesian inference. Each new measurement outcome mod- ifies the observer’s knowledge of the system and leads to a revised estimate of the system state. This interpretation emphasizes that quantum measurement theory is fundamentally concerned with the processing of information obtained from measurement records. The stochas- tic master equation governing ρt describes how information extracted from the system modifies the observer’s description of the system. In this sense the Belavkin equation plays a role analogous to classical filtering equations used in statistical signal pro- cessing. D. Neural Quantum Filters The Born perceptron architecture provides a computational mechanism capable of implementing a similar filtering pro- cess. The neural state vector xt represents the internal estimate of the system state, while the sampled outcomes it correspond to measurement observations. The update rule xt+1 = F(xt, it) (66) updates the internal state based on new observations. This structure closely resembles the update steps used in classical filtering algorithms such as the Kalman filter or particle filter. The perceptron weights determine the measurement model that maps internal states to observable outcomes. Learning these weights corresponds to identifying the measurement operators that generate the observed data. Consequently the neural architecture may be interpreted as a learnable quantum filter capable of modeling measurement processes. This perspective suggests a natural synergy between neural computation and the statistical analysis of quantum measure- ment records. E. Belavkin–Gut¸˘a Statistical Interpretation The statistical properties of quantum filtering processes have been studied extensively by Gut¸˘a and collaborators. Their work analyzes the asymptotic behavior of measurement records produced by continuously observed quantum systems and investigates the problem of estimating unknown system parameters.

In this framework the measurement record is treated as a stochastic process whose statistical properties encode informa- tion about the underlying system dynamics. By analyzing the measurement data one can infer parameters such as coupling strengths or Hamiltonian coefficients. The Born perceptron architecture generates stochastic mea- surement records through its sampling process. These records can therefore be analyzed using statistical techniques similar to those employed in quantum filtering theory. The combination of neural generative models and quantum statistical inference provides a promising framework for ana- lyzing measurement processes in complex quantum systems. F. Quantum Energy Teleportation An important application of measurement back-action arises in the phenomenon known as quantum energy teleportation (QET), introduced by Hotta. In QET protocols energy can effectively be transported between spatially separated regions of a quantum system using only local operations and classical communication. The key idea underlying QET is that local measurements can inject energy into a quantum field or many-body system through measurement back-action. Because quantum systems exhibit spatial correlations in their ground states, information obtained from a local measurement can be used to perform operations in a distant region that extract energy from the system. In a typical QET protocol two observers, traditionally referred to as Alice and Bob, interact with a shared quantum system. Alice performs a local measurement on the system at position A. The measurement injects energy into the system due to back-action. Alice then communicates the measurement result to Bob through a classical channel. Bob uses this information to perform a local operation at position B that allows him to extract energy from the system. Remarkably, the energy extracted by Bob can exceed the energy locally present in the system prior to his operation. The protocol effectively transfers energy between distant locations without any physical energy carrier traveling between them. This phenomenon does not violate energy conservation because the total energy of the system increases when Alice performs her measurement. The measurement back-action provides the energy that later becomes available to Bob. G. Back-Action and Energy Injection The QET protocol highlights the fundamental role of mea- surement back-action as a physical resource. When a mea- surement is performed on a quantum system, the interaction between the system and the measuring device can transfer energy to the system. In conventional measurement theory this effect is often regarded as a source of noise or disturbance. In QET, however, the back-action is deliberately exploited to inject energy into the system in a controlled manner. The energy injection can be analyzed by considering the expectation value of the system Hamiltonian before and after the measurement. Let H denote the Hamiltonian of the system. If ρ is the initial state and ρ′ is the post-measurement state, the energy change produced by the measurement is ∆E = Tr(Hρ′) −Tr(Hρ). (67) In many QET protocols the measurement produces a pos- itive energy injection that can later be partially extracted elsewhere in the system. H. Neural Representation of Quantum Energy Teleportation The Born perceptron framework provides a natural com- putational model for representing the measurement processes that underlie QET protocols. In this representation the neural state vector xt corresponds to the quantum state of the system, while perceptron layers represent measurement operations. Alice’s measurement corresponds to sampling an outcome iA ∼p(i|xt) (68) which modifies the neural state according to xt+1 = F(xt, iA). (69) This update represents the back-action of the measurement, which injects energy into the system. The measurement outcome is then transmitted through a classical communication channel and used to condition Bob’s operation. In the neural model this corresponds to applying a transformation xt+2 = G(xt+1, iA) (70) that depends explicitly on Alice’s measurement result. Bob’s operation extracts energy from the system by exploit- ing correlations present in the system state. The effectiveness of the extraction depends on the information contained in the measurement outcome. I. Trajectory Interpretation of QET Within the neural framework the QET protocol can be interpreted as a trajectory in which measurement outcomes guide subsequent operations. The trajectory consists of three stages: 1) Measurement at location A producing outcome iA. 2) State update due to measurement back-action. 3) Conditional operation at location B using the commu- nicated outcome. Each stage modifies the neural state vector and contributes to the overall trajectory of the system. The probability of a particular sequence of events is given by P(path) = P(iA|xt)P(xt+1|xt, iA)P(xt+2|xt+1, iA). (71) This expression highlights the sequential nature of the protocol and the dependence of later operations on earlier measurement outcomes.

J. Implications for Quantum Measurement Models The QET protocol provides a compelling example of how measurement back-action can be harnessed as a useful physical resource. Rather than treating measurement disturbance purely as a limitation, QET demonstrates that the energy injected by measurement can be used to perform useful tasks. Within the Born perceptron framework, this phenomenon appears as a consequence of the state update induced by the sampling process. The measurement outcome modifies the neural state in a way that alters the system’s energy distribution. Because the neural architecture explicitly represents mea- surement outcomes and state updates, it provides a natural computational framework for modeling protocols that exploit measurement-induced energy transfer. K. Summary The framework developed in this work connects several important ideas in quantum measurement theory and neural computation. The Born perceptron architecture provides a neural representation of measurement processes in which prob- abilistic sampling generates trajectories of outcomes. Measure- ment back-action appears as stochastic disturbances in the neu- ral state, reproducing key features of quantum measurement theory. In the continuous-time limit the sequential sampling process leads to stochastic differential equations analogous to the Belavkin filtering equation. The measurement outcomes form a stochastic record that can be analyzed using the statistical methods developed by Belavkin and Gut¸˘a. The example of quantum energy teleportation illustrates how measurement back-action can serve as a physical resource for energy transfer in correlated quantum systems. Within the neural framework this process corresponds to state updates and conditional operations guided by measurement outcomes. These connections suggest that neural architectures based on probabilistic sampling may provide useful computational tools for modeling complex quantum measurement processes and exploring new applications of measurement-induced dynamics.
