A Note on Invoking Indo-Scandinavian Cultural Affinity for Increased Cooperation

Chawla, Aman

Abstract

The North-Germanic and Indo-Aryan branches of the Proto-Indo-European (PIE) lan- guage family share a remarkable density of cognates, parallel mythological motifs, and com- parable social structures that have fascinated linguists since 1786. This paper argues that the Indo-Scandinavian linguistic connection represents an underutilized foundation for cul- tural diplomacy between India and the Nordic states. We present a four-phase framework for narrative development, institutional partnership, economic collaboration, and symbolic integration that could transform a 4,500-year-old shared linguistic heritage into a durable strategic relationship. While cultural aﬃnity cannot determine procurement outcomes or guarantee speciﬁc economic results, we argue it can provide political sustainability and pub- lic legitimacy for partnerships that serve mutual strategic interests. Drawing on precedents from cultural diplomacy initiatives worldwide, we suggest that modest, sustained invest- ment in Indo-Nordic cultural programming (estimated $250–350 million over 2026–2030) could materially strengthen bilateral relations and create conditions favorable to expanded cooperation across multiple domains. Keywords: Proto-Indo-European, linguistic aﬃnity, cultural proximity, cooperation and cul- ture, Vedic Sanskrit Introduction When William Jones delivered his Third Anniversary Discourse to the Asiatic Society in 1786, declaring that Sanskrit, Greek, and Latin must have “sprung from some common source, which, perhaps, no longer exists,”1 he established the foundation for comparative Indo-European stud- ies. Two centuries of scholarship have revealed that among the most linguistically intimate branches of this family are Vedic Sanskrit and Old Norse: two cultures separated by millennia and thousands of kilometers, yet preserving strikingly similar vocabulary in domains of kinship, mythology, and social organization.[7] 1Jones ([1]), Asiatick Researches 1:415–431. 1

This paper examines whether the Indo-Scandinavian PIE connection can be deliberately de- veloped as cultural infrastructure supporting closer bilateral relations. Unlike more distant or contested linguistic relationships within the Indo-European family, the Vedic–Norse continuum oﬀers accessible, emotionally resonant parallels: thunder-gods wielding cognate weapons against cognate serpents, cosmic trees with cognate names, and tri-functional social orders.[2] We pro- pose a sequenced cultural diplomacy program designed to convert this linguistic reality into a foundation for expanded strategic cooperation. Before outlining this four-phase framework, we ﬁrst establish the empirical basis for the claimed linguistic and mythological parallels. Organization of the Paper. This paper is structured to guide the reader from linguis- tic foundations to strategic implications of Indo-Scandinavian cultural diplomacy. Section 2 (The Linguistic Foundation) establishes the empirical basis for Indo-Aryan and North-Germanic aﬃnities, highlighting high-recognition cognates and mythological parallels. Section 3 (Phase I: Narrative Development (2026–2028)) proposes ﬂagship media production and audience en- gagement strategies to seed public awareness. Section 4 (Phase II: Institutional Development (2027–2030)) outlines academic infrastructure, educational exchanges, and public festivals to embed cultural aﬃnity in durable organizational forms. Section 5 (Phase III: Economic and Strategic Cooperation (2028–2035)) explores how cultural familiarity can support defense, en- ergy, and governance partnerships, while clarifying its role as a complementary, not causal, factor. Section 6 (Phase IV: Symbolic Integration (2030 onwards)) envisions permanent expres- sions of partnership through twin-city programs, youth events, and joint exercises. Section 7 (Strategic Assessment) evaluates the comparative advantages, constraints, and realistic projec- tions of Indo-Nordic cooperation. Finally, Section 8 (Conclusion) synthesizes the argument, emphasizing the opportunity to convert shared linguistic heritage into a resilient 21st-century alliance architecture. The Linguistic Foundation The strength of any cultural diplomacy initiative depends on the authenticity and accessibility of the shared heritage it invokes. The structural correspondence between Indra–Vtra and Þórr–Miðgarðsormr narratives,2 the cosmic bovine ﬁgures (Auðhumla and Kmadhenu),[10] and World-Tree concepts (Avattha and Yggdrasill)[10, 8] provide accessible entry points for popular cultural content that could reach audiences beyond academic specialists. These mythological parallels are especially signiﬁcant because they suggest not merely borrowed words but shared conceptual architec- turesentire systems of understanding the divine, the cosmic, and the heroic that evolved from common origins.[8] Having established the authenticity and accessibility of the shared heritage, we now turn to the practical question of activation. How can linguistic facts documented in scholarly journals be transformed into public consciousness and, ultimately, strategic partnerships? The answer lies in a carefully sequenced program beginning with narrative development. Phase I: Narrative Development (2026–2028) Cultural aﬃnity cannot be manufactured through government declarations or academic con- ferences alone. It requires compelling stories that reach mass audiences and create emotional connections. The ﬁrst phase therefore focuses on ﬂagship media production capable of intro- ducing the Indo-Scandinavian connection to millions of households. 3.1 Flagship Media Production A co-produced streaming series developed jointly by Nordic public broadcasters (SVT, NRK, DR, YLE) and major Indian production houses could serve as an anchor for broader cultural programming. Production budgets for comparable international co-productions range from $100–150 million. Precedent: Turkey’s Dirili: Erturul (2014–2019) demonstrated that well-produced historical drama can generate measurable soft power returns and tourism increases, though precise quan- 2See especially [9] and [8]. 4

tiﬁcation remains methodologically challenging.3 The Turkish example is instructive not be- cause India and the Nordic states should replicate its speciﬁc approach, but because it demon- strates that cultural content can create genuine public aﬃnity where little previously existed.[11] 3.2 Realistic Audience Targets Drawing on penetration rates achieved by successful Nordic drama exports (Vikings, The Bridge) and Indian streaming content reaching international audiences (Sacred Games, Delhi Crime), a well-distributed co-production could reasonably target: • 25–40% household reach in Nordic markets within 36 months • 50–80 million viewers in Indian markets • Signiﬁcant secondary social media engagement, though viral adoption patterns remain inherently unpredictable Success would depend critically on distribution platform commitments, marketing investment, and narrative quality. Importantly, the goal is not merely entertainment but the creation of shared cultural reference points that subsequent phases can build upon.[11] With popular interest established through media production, the second phase shifts focus to institutional infrastructure that can sustain and deepen the connection across generations. Phase II: Institutional Development (2027–2030) Media content creates awareness, but lasting relationships require institutional foundations. Phase II therefore emphasizes academic infrastructure, educational exchanges, and public en- gagement programs that embed Indo-Nordic connections in permanent organizational struc- tures. 4.1 Academic Infrastructure Establishment of joint research positions and teaching programs: • Five Indo-Nordic Heritage research chairs at Uppsala, Copenhagen, Oslo, JNU, and IIT Delhi with joint funding (estimated 60–80 million over 2027–2032) • Annual academic exchange program for 1,500–2,000 secondary and tertiary students • Integration of comparative Indo-European content into existing cultural exchange pro- grams These academic positions serve multiple functions: they generate ongoing research validating the connection, train cohorts of students familiar with both cultures, and provide authoritative voices for public commentary when bilateral issues arise. 3Estimates of economic impact vary widely; Turkish cultural oﬃcials cited ﬁgures exceeding $1 billion annually in tourism and trade facilitation during peak broadcast years. 5

4.2 Public Engagement Beyond academic circles, institutional development must reach general publics through accessi- ble cultural programming. Expansion of existing diaspora cultural events into broader public festivals oﬀers an eﬃcient entry point. The Stockholm Mela, currently attracting approximately 25,000 visitors, could be repositioned as a ﬂagship Indo-Nordic cultural event with government support targeting 150,000–200,000 participants by 2032. Similar festivals could be established in Copenhagen, Oslo, and Indian metropolitan centers, creating annual occasions for cultural exchange that reinforce the themes introduced through media content. 4.3 Measurable Outcomes Rather than projecting speciﬁc percentage increases, we propose establishing baseline awareness metrics in 2026 through representative surveys, with interim assessment at 30-month intervals. Key indicators would include: • Recognition of Indo-European linguistic connections among target demographics • Attitudes toward bilateral cooperation • Knowledge of historical and cultural parallels These metrics allow program managers to assess eﬀectiveness and adjust strategies, while provid- ing governments with evidence-based justiﬁcation for continued investment. As public awareness grows and institutional relationships mature, conditions become favorable for the third phase: translating cultural aﬃnity into economic and strategic cooperation. Phase III: Economic and Strategic Cooperation (2028–2035) The ultimate test of cultural diplomacy lies not in sentiment surveys but in material outcomes. Phase III examines how sustained cultural programming creates conditions favorable to ex- panded economic and defense cooperation, while carefully delimiting the causal claims we can reasonably make. 5.1 The Role of Cultural Aﬃnity Cultural programming cannot determine complex procurement decisions, which depend on tech- nical speciﬁcations, cost-beneﬁt analysis, industrial oﬀset requirements, and geopolitical consid- erations. However, sustained public familiarity and positive associations can provide several strategic advantages: • Political sustainability: Elected oﬃcials face lower domestic resistance to partnerships enjoying public recognition and approval • Bureaucratic facilitation: Cultural familiarity reduces transaction costs in complex nego- tiations 6

• Long-term stability: Relationships embedded in public consciousness prove more resilient to short-term political volatility In essence, cultural aﬃnity does not make uneconomical deals viable, but it removes political friction from partnerships that serve mutual interests. This distinction is critical for realistic assessment.[5] 5.2 Potential Cooperation Domains Both India and Nordic states have identiﬁed defense-industrial cooperation as a strategic priority independent of cultural considerations. These beneﬁts accumulate over time, making Phase IV’s permanent symbolic integration both feasible and valuable. 7

6 Phase IV: Symbolic Integration (2030 onwards) If the ﬁrst three phases succeed in generating public awareness, institutional relationships, and material cooperation, Phase IV seeks to make Indo-Nordic partnership a permanent feature of both regions’ international identities through symbolic anchoring. Permanent institutional expressions of partnership: • Twin-city partnerships linking Nordic and Indian municipalities with historical or the- matic connections (e.g., Uppsala–Ujjain for astronomical heritage, Trondheim–Thanesar for solar temple traditions) • Biennial Indo-Nordic youth cultural and athletic events • Joint military exercises emphasizing interoperability and Arctic/Himalayan expertise ex- change (e.g., Exercise Northern Roots, alternating between Ladakh and Finnmark) These symbolic investments serve practical functionsmilitary exercises develop real interoperabil- ity, twin cities facilitate municipal knowledge exchangebut their primary value lies in creating recurring occasions that continuously regenerate public awareness and political commitment. A relationship embedded in annual rituals, permanent institutional ties, and shared symbols becomes resistant to the electoral cycles and budget pressures that terminate less-embedded partnerships. Having outlined the four-phase framework, we now step back to assess the strategic logic holis- tically and acknowledge the genuine constraints and uncertainties involved. Strategic Assessment Any proposal for sustained diplomatic investment must address two questions: why these speciﬁc partners, and what realistic outcomes can be expected? This section provides comparative context and acknowledges limitations. 7.1 Comparative Advantages The Nordic states oﬀer India several strategic beneﬁts relative to other potential partners: • Political predictability: Consensus-based governance systems produce stable, long-term policy commitments • Technology transfer willingness: History of oﬀset agreements and co-development arrange- ments • Non-alignment compatibility: No competing great-power entanglements that complicate Indian strategic autonomy • Values alignment: Shared commitment to democratic governance and rules-based interna- tional order[12] 8

These structural advantages explain why Indo-Nordic cooperation makes strategic sense inde- pendent of cultural programming.[12] The question cultural diplomacy addresses is not whether partnership is desirable, but how to overcome the barriers of geographic distance and mutual unfamiliarity that have historically prevented it from developing. 7.2 Challenges and Limitations Realistic assessment requires acknowledging factors that constrain partnership potential regard- less of cultural programming: • Scale asymmetry: Combined Nordic GDP ($1.7 trillion) represents only 40% of India’s economy • Geographic distance: Physical separation limits certain forms of economic integration • Competing priorities: Both regions maintain more proximate strategic concerns (EU inte- gration for Nordics, South Asian security for India) • Resource constraints: Cultural programming competes with other diplomatic priorities for limited budgets These limitations suggest that even successful cultural diplomacy cannot make the Nordic states India’s primary international partner, nor can it substitute for core national interests. 7.3 Realistic Projection With sustained investment and favorable conditions, Nordic states could plausibly emerge as a signiﬁcant (though not primary) defense and technology partner for India by 2035.[13] The relationship would complement rather than replace India’s partnerships with France, the United States, Russia, and Israel. Cultural programming creates permissive conditions for expanded cooperation but cannot substitute for alignment of material interests. The achievable outcome is a relationship that consistently punches above its weight relative to economic scale and geographic proximityvaluable precisely because most partnerships remain constrained by those factors. Conclusion The Proto-Indo-European linguistic heritage linking India and Scandinavia represents an un- usual opportunity in contemporary cultural diplomacy: a genuine, academically grounded con- nection that remains largely unknown to general publics in both regions. Unlike invented or exaggerated historical ties, the Vedic–Norse parallels can withstand scholarly scrutiny while providing accessible content for popular consumption.[8, 7] We propose that a coordinated cultural investment of $250–350 million distributed across me- dia production, academic programming, and public engagement (2026–2030) could materially strengthen Indo-Nordic bilateral relations. While precise economic returns remain inherently 9

uncertain, precedents from Turkish, Korean, and Japanese cultural diplomacy suggest that well- executed programs generate returns signiﬁcantly exceeding direct costs through enhanced trade, tourism, and political cooperation.[11] For India speciﬁcally, deepening cooperation with Nordic partners in clean energy technology, sustainable urban infrastructure, and advanced manufac- turing equipment could prove critical to achieving the Viksit Bharat 2047 objectives, as Nordic expertise in these domains aligns precisely with India’s developmental priorities for becoming a developed nation.[14] In an international environment where narrative infrastructure increasingly shapes the feasibility of material partnerships, shared linguistic heritage may provide a foundation for a durable Indo- Nordic strategic relationship. The oldest documented language family may yet prove relevant to 21st-century alliance architecture not through deterministic causation, but by creating cultural conditions that make expanded cooperation politically sustainable and publicly legitimate. The question is not whether Indo-Scandinavian cultural aﬃnity can be activated, but whether both regions will recognize the opportunity before it passes.
