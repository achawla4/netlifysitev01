Yajurveda · A traditional śākhā-vṛkṣa chart

The Yajurveda Tree

सप्तविंशतिशाखात्मको यजुर्वेदवृक्षः — a printed genealogical chart that traces how the teaching of the Yajurveda, beginning from a single teacher, opened out into twenty-seven named branches (śākhās).

For the curious reader · a guide to reading the chart, not a substitute for it
Begin here

What is the Yajurveda-vṛkṣa?

The page reproduced here (folio [७], "seven") is a vṛkṣa — literally a "tree" — a diagram used in traditional Sanskrit scholarship to display a lineage of teaching as a trunk that splits into branches. This particular tree is titled "Saptaviṃśati-śākhātmako Yajurveda-vṛkṣaḥ", "the Yajurveda-tree consisting of twenty-seven śākhās." Its root is a single teacher; its branches are the many schools that later Sanskrit literature remembers as transmitting the Yajurveda, each usually named after a teacher, a family, or a place.

Charts of this kind belong to the same family of literature as the Caraṇa-vyūha texts, which enumerate the branches (śākhās) of each Veda. A footnote on the page itself states that its arrangement follows a "śākhā-paṭṭa-prakaraṇa-vyūha," matched against the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa — so the chart is best read as a visual summary of an older prose enumeration, not as an independent historical record.

Its purpose

A teaching lineage, drawn as a tree

One root teacher at the base; the trunk and branches carry the names of the schools said to descend from him, down to the smallest regional offshoot.

Its arithmetic

27, shown as its own sum

The chart totals its branches for the reader: 12 + 7 + 5 + 4 = 28, minus 1 for a branch-name that is shared and not counted twice — leaving 27.

Its source

Purāṇic enumeration

A footnote credits the "śākhā-paṭṭa-prakaraṇa-vyūha" of the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa as the authority behind the chart's arrangement of names.

Where the tree begins

The root: a single teacher

At the base of the tree, numbered "1," sits the teacher from whom every branch is said to descend:

सकलयजुर्वेदाध्यापकः वैशम्पायन-पराभिधश्रकाचार्यः ।
"Teacher of the entire Yajurveda: Vaiśampāyana — also known by another name — the ācārya."
Base of the tree, folio [७]

Sanskrit tradition remembers Vaiśampāyana as the teacher through whom the Yajurveda was first taught to a group of pupils — the same narrative context in which the later split between the "Black" (Kṛṣṇa) and "White" (Śukla) Yajurveda is traditionally told, with Yājñavalkya breaking away and the remaining pupils, in the shape of tittiri partridges, absorbing the scattered teaching that became the Taittirīya tradition. This chart does not narrate that story; it simply takes Vaiśampāyana as the trunk from which all twenty-seven named branches grow.

A cautious note on the second name. The compound "para-abhidha-śrakācārya" printed after Vaiśampāyana's name is not fully certain in this transcription — the print is small and partly worn. Readers working from the original should treat the second epithet as unconfirmed until checked against the plate.

The arithmetic, spelled out on the page: 12 + 7 + 5 + 4 = 28. But one name — Hāridravīyāḥ, marked with a "×" — appears in both the four-branch group and the seven-branch (Mānava) group. A footnote explains that a branch marked this way "is one and the same, not a separate one" (ekaḥ eva, na bhinnaḥ), so it is subtracted once: 28 − 1 = 27.

Two of the four groups also carry a place-name for each branch, which the page's footnote says follows "the region-indications of the Veda-vṛkṣa chart" — that is, a companion chart rather than independent geographical knowledge:

A branch that grows further

Kaṭha's own thirteen regional forms

A separate, larger box on the chart lists thirteen further names, each tied to a region — and here the geography is not only earthly. Alongside real places like Kaśmīra and Siṃhala (Sri Lanka), several branches are assigned to dvīpas, the concentric mythic "continents" of Purāṇic cosmology (Krauñca-dvīpa, Śāka-dvīpa, Kuśa-dvīpa, Śveta-dvīpa), and one to "Yavana-deśa" — the Greek-influenced West of Purāṇic geography. A footnote explains why this box stands apart from the main count of 27:

कठानां शाखाभेदाद् द्वादशभेदाः । देशभेदात् त्रयोदश पुनरवान्तरभेदा जाता इति दृश्यते ।
"By difference of branch, the Kaṭhas have twelve divisions [the Caraka group above]. By difference of region, thirteen further sub-branches are seen to have arisen."
Footnote, folio [७]

A useful distinction

One traditional count among several

Different Sanskrit sources do not always agree on how many śākhās the Yajurveda once had — some enumerations run much higher than 27, and the surviving, actively transmitted schools today (chiefly Taittirīya and Śukla/Vājasaneyi, with the Kāṭhaka and Maitrāyaṇī preserved mainly as texts) are far fewer than either number. This chart represents one particular traditional counting, drawn from a specific Purāṇic enumeration, and should be read as such rather than as a claim that all twenty-seven schools survived as living traditions into modern times.

A suggestion for the seeker: Trace one branch from root to tip — say, Hāridravīyāḥ, marked "×" and shared between two groups. Ask what it means for a single teaching lineage to be claimed by two different branches at once. That question — how a "family tree" of texts differs from a family tree of people — is a good way into thinking about how oral, teacher-to-pupil transmission actually branches.
